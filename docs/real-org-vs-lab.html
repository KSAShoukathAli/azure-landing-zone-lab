Azure Landing Zone Lab - reference

A real landing zone, and how this lab stands in for it

The CAF hierarchy from the tenant down, what each level is for, what the lab uses instead, and why. Written 2026-11-07, after 7.1A.

The interviewer says: "Walk me through your landing zone." You start with the hub resource group. They stop you. "Resource group? Where are your management groups? Which subscription is the hub in?"

The lab is built in one MSDN subscription, so a lot of the real structure is played by resource groups. That's fine, as long as you can say what each piece stands for, and what you'd do differently with a real tenant. That's this page.

1. How a real organization is laid out

One hierarchy, from the tenant down. Anything assigned at a level, Azure Policy or Azure RBAC, flows down to everything below it, and a child can't remove what a parent assigned.

LevelWhat it is forTypical assignments
Tenant (Entra ID)The identity directory: users, groups, apps, service principals. Everything hangs off itConditional Access, PIM
Top management groupCompany-wide rulesAllowed regions, required tags, Defender for Cloud on
Platform, then Connectivity / Identity / ManagementShared services run by the platform teamConnectivity may have public IPs (it's the internet edge). Management holds Log Analytics
Landing Zones, then Corp / OnlineWhere app teams live. Corp is internal, peered to the hub. Online is internet-facingCorp: no public IPs, private endpoints only, diagnostics to Log Analytics
SubscriptionOne landing zone: billing, quota and blast-radius boundaryBudgets, the app team's RBAC
Resource groupLifecycle and ownership folder inside a subscriptionRarely policy. Sometimes an exemption
Two hierarchies, not one The resource hierarchy above decides governance. Billing is a separate tree (billing account, billing profile, invoice section, subscription) that decides who pays. A subscription sits in both. Management groups aren't only for policy: RBAC and cost views flow down them too.

4. The decisions, and why

Why resource groups instead of management groups

One subscription can only sit in one management group, and this one holds your other labs too. A management group Deny would reach them all. And the real point of the tree, putting the hub and the landing zones in different branches, needs at least two subscriptions. RG scope keeps every rule inside the lab.

Why the hub owns the DNS zones

One privatelink.* zone per service, shared by every spoke. A VNet can link only one zone of a given name, so per-spoke copies would leave each VNet seeing half the records.

Why two RGs per landing zone

An RG is a lifecycle and access boundary. The network (VNet, subnets, NSGs) belongs to platform, and the services belong to the team. A subnet must live with its VNet. A private endpoint lives and dies with its service, so it goes with the service.

Why platform provisions the subnets

The workload only needs to join a subnet. Network Contributor on the VNet would also let it change the peering. So platform creates subnets to the team's requirements, and the workload gets a join-only custom role.

Why guardrails moved ahead of 7.1C

From the moment 7.1A created the RG, it had no rules, and 7.1B was about to create NSGs in it. In a real tenant this gap never opens: a new subscription under Corp inherits the rules the instant it lands.

5. If this were a real tenant

Three changes, in order.

ChangeWhat it buys
Build the management group tree: Contoso, Platform and Connectivity, Landing Zones and CorpRules are inherited instead of assigned per RG. New landing zones are governed the second they land
Separate subscriptions: one for the hub, one per landing zoneReal billing, quota and blast-radius boundaries. The hub exemption comes from its branch, not from a filter in code
Assign one initiative at Corp instead of three policies per RGOne assignment to manage. Adding a rule changes every landing zone at once

A safe way to practice the first one in this subscription: build the tree empty, move the subscription under Corp, and assign policies there in Audit mode. Audit only reports, so your other labs keep working while you watch inheritance in the Compliance view.

Interview line

"In a real tenant I'd follow the CAF tree: Platform with Connectivity, Identity and Management, and Landing Zones with Corp and Online, one subscription per landing zone, and policy initiatives assigned at the management groups so new subscriptions inherit them. My lab runs in a single shared subscription, so resource groups stand in for subscriptions and policies are assigned per RG, with the hub exempt in code instead of by branch. The ownership model is the same: platform owns the hub, the shared DNS zones, the spoke networks and the guardrails; the integration team owns its workload RG and deploys it through its own pipeline."
